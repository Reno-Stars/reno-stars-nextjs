What Does "Whole-Home Renovation" Actually Include?

Whole-home renovation means refreshing multiple rooms or systems in a single project, rather than doing one room at a time. Bundling the work saves roughly 15–25% compared to doing each room separately because contractors mobilize once, material pricing is negotiated in volume, and you negotiate only one permit cycle.

What Drives Cost Up in a Whole-Home Reno?

  • Condo age: Buildings from the 1970s–1990s often have asbestos in flooring glue or wall texture. Abatement adds $2,000–$8,000.
  • Strata approvals: Most Vancouver and Richmond condos require strata approval for changes to fixtures, flooring, and anything affecting common elements. Budget 4–8 weeks for approval and $500–$2,000 in application fees.
  • Kitchen scope: Custom cabinetry ($15,000–$40,000 alone) is the single largest kitchen line item. Refacing or reglazing is roughly half the cost of new custom cabinets.
  • Bathroom count: Each additional bathroom adds $10,000–$60,000 depending on fixture grade. A powder room refresh runs $3,500–$4,000; a master bath with curbless shower can hit $40,000+.
  • Electrical panel upgrades: Homes with 60-amp panels need an upgrade to 100 or 200 amps before major renos. $3,000–$8,000.
  • Flooring: Hardwood throughout a 1,200 sq ft home: $6,000–$12,000 installed. Engineered wood or LVP: $4,000–$9,000.
  • Permit fees: A Vancouver renovation permit ranges from $1,500 to $5,000+ depending on project valuation. Structural changes or additions require a professional engineer, adding $2,000–$10,000.

Timeline: How Long Does a Whole-Home Reno Take?

Duration depends on the property type and scope:

  • Condo whole-home refresh: 1–5 weeks. Most work is surface-level — flooring, paint, bathroom fixtures, kitchen cabinet refinishing.
  • Detached house full renovation: 6–16 weeks. Involves multiple trades, potential structural work, and mandatory permit timelines of 4–8 weeks for structural changes.
  • WFH office conversion: 1 week. Framing, electrical, and glass partition installation — fastest whole-home sub-project.

Delays are most common in: stratas holding up approvals (4–8 weeks), supply chain for specialty fixtures (2–4 weeks), and structural engineer sign-offs on old houses with unknown framing conditions.

Should You Do a Whole-Home Reno or Room by Room?

Room by room is lower-disruption in the short term. Whole-home is cheaper in the long term. The 15–25% savings from bundling typically outweighs the inconvenience of living through a single disruption instead of multiple ones. If you're already planning to do three or more rooms, a whole-home package almost always wins on cost. If you have a tight budget and only one urgent room, do that room first and plan the rest.

Whole-Home Renovation in Vancouver: Frequently Asked Questions

What is the average cost of a whole-home renovation in Vancouver?

Based on Reno Stars' completed projects, a whole-home renovation in Vancouver ranges from $23,000 for a condo refresh to $31,000+ for a multi-room renovation with custom cabinetry. Full custom renovations of detached houses typically run $80,000–$200,000+. Budget range depends on property age, number of rooms, and finish level.

Do I need a permit for a whole-home renovation in Vancouver?

Yes, for any work that changes the structure, electrical system, plumbing layout, or exterior appearance of a Vancouver property. Interior cosmetic updates — flooring, painting, bathroom fixtures without plumbing relocation — may not require a permit. A renovation permit in Vancouver costs $1,500–$5,000+ and takes 4–8 weeks to process for structural work. Your contractor should include permit acquisition in their scope.

How long does a whole-home renovation take?

A condo whole-home refresh takes 1–5 weeks. A detached house full renovation ranges from 6–16 weeks depending on scope. Structural changes requiring an engineer's review add 4–8 weeks to permit timelines. Reno Stars provides a detailed schedule at the quote stage, updated weekly during the project.

Should I renovate my whole house at once or one room at a time?

Whole-house at once saves 15–25% through bundled material pricing, single permit cycle, and one contractor mobilization. The main drawback is living through the disruption without a finished room to retreat to. If your budget is limited, renovating in phases is viable — just sequence the highest-disruption room (kitchen or bathroom) last so you retain living functionality throughout.
